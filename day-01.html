The Unruly Learner

The first three harmonics — on curiosity, on talent, and on the stubbornness of a dream.

By Pravinkumar Ghodake

I keep a notebook of lines that have changed how I work. Not a journal — nothing so organized. More like a set of boundary conditions I return to when the field around me gets noisy. This blog is that notebook, opened up. Three lines a day, and whatever the lines make me think about my own work and my own life. The physics will do what it always does: it will find its way into everything, because that is what physics does once you have lived with it long enough.

Starting here, with the three that got me through the early years.

I Harmonic I

The Unruly Learner

Study hard what interests you the most in the most undisciplined, irreverent and original manner possible.

— Richard Feynman Science

I did not come to research through a clean corridor. I came to it the way most independent people do — through a question I couldn't put down, and through the slow realization that nobody was going to hand me a curriculum for it.

Feynman's line is the permission slip I wish I'd been given earlier. Look at what he actually says: not study hard, but study hard what interests you most. Then he tells you how — undisciplined, irreverent, original. The discipline is in the effort. The irreverence is in the refusal to let a syllabus decide what is worth knowing.

Somewhere along the way I stopped waiting. I built a Google Site, page by page, because I needed somewhere to put the questions. I opened a GitHub repository for the academic archive because I wanted a record that belonged to no institution but me. I started putting things on Figshare because a DOI is a small, durable fact, and small durable facts accumulate. None of this was assigned. None of it was asked for. That is exactly the point — the infrastructure is built by the question, not by the committee.

The unruly part is not a personality trait. It is a method. In nonlinear dynamics, the interesting behavior rarely shows up under the standard approximation. You have to leave the linear path to see the overtones. Curiosity works the same way. Follow what pulls you, even if the path is not on anyone's map.

II Harmonic II

The Engine Is Not Talent

I have no special talent. I am only passionately curious.

— Albert Einstein Science

I used to believe the opposite. I believed that the people whose work I admired had something I didn't — a different starting material, a better initial condition. And that belief was comfortable, because it excused me from trying.

Then I noticed something about the researchers I actually respected. They were not the brightest people in the room. They were the ones who kept returning to the question. After the seventh failed mesh convergence. After the third rejected manuscript. After the first year where nobody cited the paper they'd spent months on. They did not have a higher ceiling. They had a longer fuse.

Talent is a static property. Curiosity is a continuous forcing function. A system with modest baseline parameters will still reach enormous displacement if you drive it persistently at its natural frequency. I have come to trust that more than I trust any comparison of raw ability.

I write this with the usual caveat: the line is widely attributed to Einstein and I have never found the original interview it comes from, so I treat it as his stated view rather than a documented sentence. But the truth of it does not depend on the attribution. It depends on whether you have watched it play out in your own life. I have. The people who do original work are almost never the most gifted. They are the ones who did not stop oscillating.

III Harmonic III

Protect the Signal

Don't ever let somebody tell you... You can't do something. All right? You got a dream... You gotta protect it. People can't do somethin' themselves, they wanna tell you you can't do it. If you want somethin', go get it. Period.

— Chris Gardner, The Pursuit of Happyness (2006) Film

This is the line people quote as motivation. I don't read it that way. I read it as a statement about signal propagation through a lossy medium.

Every environment you enter introduces attenuation, scattering, and noise. Some of it is accidental. Some of it is the ordinary damped response of people who have their own ceiling and mistake it for yours. Chris Gardner's line is not believe in yourself. It is protect the phase. Defend the boundary conditions of your own focus against the ambient interference of other people's limits.

I have been on the receiving end of that ambient interference often enough to know it is not usually malicious. It is projection. Someone looks at what you are trying to do, and they run the calculation against their own parameters, and they conclude impossible. The calculation is correct. The parameters are theirs.

The dream is not a wish. It is a system that requires boundary conditions, and you are the one who has to impose them. The people who tell you that you can't are not measuring your ceiling. They are measuring their own. Do not confuse the two.

The film earns the line because it is not about talent. It is about an unpaid internship, a sleeping-in-a-bathroom stall, and the daily, unglamorous decision to keep showing up. That is what go get it actually looks like. The dream is protected by attendance. Not by conviction. By showing up, again, on the days the field is noisy.

These three are the opening chord. Feynman says follow what interests you. Einstein says the engine is curiosity, not talent. Gardner says protect it, because the medium will damp you. Put together, they describe the only method I have ever found that works: a coherent source, driven at its natural frequency, with the boundaries defended against noise.

Everything else in this notebook is a variation on that.
